Adversarily challenge and verify these independent review findings on what needs to happen before implementation.
Review:
  Before implementation starts, you need a whole-Console roadmap and an executable, independently reviewed package for the first slice. Every later feature does not need a finished specification, but its existence,
    dependencies, and architectural implications must be accounted for.

    This is the complete readiness checklist I would use—not a claim that the current repository already satisfies it.

    ### 1. Whole-Console capability and dependency map

    Record the entire intended product:

    - Foundry-equivalent capabilities, explicitly mapping Ontology, Actions, Fusion, and the broader suite.
    - HR, attendance, labor, payroll, and employment lifecycle workflows.
    - Shared approvals, work assignment and handoff, messenger, mail, calendar, storage, and collaborative spreadsheets.
    - Applicant, former employee, partner, and external sharing experiences.
    - Platform administration, integrations, security, and operations.

    Each capability needs its dependencies, delivery phase, acceptance outcome, and status. Deferred capabilities remain visible. This must align with the authoritative product, roadmap, and delivery documents.

    ### 2. Shared architectural contracts

    Settle the foundations that early implementation would otherwise accidentally define:

    - Persistent account identity, tenancy, and within-tenant company/group/site/department scopes.
    - Entity identity, typed properties, relationships, schema evolution, and provenance.
    - Draft identity, change history, replay, snapshots, and finalization.
    - Actions, validation, approvals, idempotency, audit, and external side effects.
    - Work ownership, delegation, reassignment, and continuity.
    - Resource-, field-, and action-level authorization with Cedar.

    Distinguish committed contracts, replaceable implementation choices, and unresolved decisions. Avoid freezing every storage or concurrency choice prematurely.

    ### 3. UI and interaction architecture

    Define enough of the Leptos SSR experience to prevent disconnected modules:

    - Navigation that scales across the whole Console.
    - Reusable entity views and consistent action entry points.
    - Dedicated communication surfaces connected to business objects.
    - Policy-derived visibility and server-enforced actions.
    - Input, validation, submission, approval, tracking, and recovery flows.
    - Accessibility, keyboard use, loading, empty, error, and stale-data states.
    - Essential information surfaced where decisions happen.

    Specify the SSR/hydration boundary and what genuinely requires live collaboration.

    ### 4. First complete workflow specification

    Choose a bounded slice with explicit entry and completion conditions.

    Describe its actual users, inputs, screens, state transitions, rules, outputs, and downstream effects. Include exceptions, cancellation, correction, retries, manual intervention, and handoff.

    For HR/payroll, distinguish verified legal requirements from assumptions and identify rules requiring authoritative confirmation.

    ### 5. Security and information-flow model

    Define and review:

    - Cross-tenant isolation and separately governed intra-tenant access.
    - Field redaction, entity links, search results, notifications, exports, and caches.
    - SSR responses, hydration data, subscriptions, logs, telemetry, and outbound integrations.
    - Permission revocation during an active session or pending action.
    - Narrow, time-bound external sharing and its audit trail.

    Identify which properties receive executable tests, Verus proofs, or operational controls—and the limits of each guarantee.

    ### 6. Workload and concurrency plan

    Use the one-tenant, six-company, 2,000-person pilot as the initial operating baseline. Record assumptions for active users, concurrent editors, data volume, and workload mix.

    Also define:

    - Hot-object and hot-cell contention scenarios.
    - Conflict semantics, ordering, fairness, cancellation, and retry behavior.
    - Latency, throughput, queue, and resource limits.
    - Experiments comparing plausible mechanisms rather than assuming locks or MVCC.
    - What must change at 50,000 and 1,000,000 employees.

    Those larger scales need an explicit evaluation path; they do not need production certification before the first slice.

    ### 7. Operations and recovery contracts

    For the first slice, specify deployment ownership, topology assumptions, migrations, backup/restore, failure detection, recovery objectives, reconciliation, and rollback.

    Make commit ambiguity, duplicate delivery, partial failure, and external side-effect recovery explicit. Schedule larger operational exercises before the exposure they protect.

    ### 8. Executable production-standard TDD package

    Acceptance criteria must map to runnable tests with trustworthy fixtures and oracles.

    Cover the slice’s relevant domain rules, authorization, persistence, concurrency, recovery, and browser behavior. Record:

    - Exact commands and discovered/executed counts.
    - Why each expected failure occurs.
    - Behavior blocked behind earlier failures.
    - Coverage gaps and proof boundaries.

    A suite failing entirely at a missing schema does not yet demonstrate that its deeper assertions are meaningful.

    ### 9. Reproducible implementation environment

    Verify the toolchain and dependency compatibility, build commands, database setup, CI requirements, and Leptos SSR test path.

    Confirm any proposed version pin actually exists and works. Resolve prerequisite build failures so implementation failures are distinguishable from infrastructure failures.

    ### 10. Consolidated independent review

    Review the same exact candidate across architecture, UX, security, domain correctness, concurrency, recovery, and test quality.

    Resolve blocking findings. Record evidence and rationale for accepted findings, outstanding HOLDs, and deferred work. Reconcile design, tests, roadmap, and implementation instructions after revisions.

    ### 11. Concrete implementation handoff

    The final package needs:

    - Exact scope, base SHA, ownership, and mechanical implementation guide.
    - An executable red probe on the clean tree.
    - The same probe as the success condition.
    - Pre-mortem, blast radius, detection, rollback, and stop conditions.
    - Review identities and explicit remaining HOLDs.
    - Admission through the repository’s prescribed lane tooling.

    Once these are satisfied for the first slice, implementation should start. Whole-Console planning establishes coverage and direction; each subsequent slice repeats the detailed design, test, and review gate.
    Production launch remains a separate evidence-based decision.
1. Give every requirement a gate. Explicitly classify it as required before implementation, before slice acceptance, or before production exposure. Later capabilities need architectural consideration now; their
     complete tests, operational exercises, and deployment certification generally belong later.

  2. Clarify what “executable TDD package” means before implementation. Author and independently review the slice’s acceptance tests, fixtures, and oracles. Execute what existing interfaces permit and identify
     genuine prerequisite failures. Deeper tests may remain blocked until their owners exist. Each implementation lane needs a meaningful RED at its actual boundary; a schema failure cannot admit unrelated
     authorization implementation. Never introduce fake implementations merely to manufacture RED.

  3. Add architectural dependency checks, not just a capability inventory. For each deferred capability, identify what the first slice must preserve: identity, ownership, policy context, versioning, event contracts,
     or data addressing. Explain whether later adoption is additive or requires migration. Otherwise, a comprehensive roadmap can still conceal an architecture that makes later features prohibitively expensive.

  4. Make authority over policy itself explicit. Resource, field, and action authorization is incomplete without rules for creating grants, changing policies, delegating authority, administering schemas, and using
     operational access. Cover privilege escalation, stale decisions, revocation, and attribution. Job titles and organizational hierarchy must not silently become authorization authority.

  5. Expand migration and lifecycle coverage. Include existing-data conversion, mixed-version readers and writers, resumable backfills, compatibility windows, and irreversible transition points. Distinguish rolling
     back software from restoring data. Persistent login identity must also coexist with retention, deletion, legal holds, and historical attribution.

  6. Separate correctness invariants from measured budgets. Isolation, attribution, and duplicate-effect prevention must hold under contention and overload. Latency and throughput have measured operating envelopes.
     For larger scales, specify the experiments and results that would trigger architectural changes; do not presume those changes are necessary based on employee count alone.

  7. Add observable usability acceptance. Listing screens and states does not demonstrate an intuitive experience. Specify representative tasks and assess whether users can discover the action, understand its
     consequences, complete it, and recover without undocumented knowledge. Measure task success, errors, assistance required, and whether essential decision information was actually presented. Numerical targets can
     remain explicit hypotheses until evaluated.

  8. Require independent review of the evidence-generating machinery. Review fixtures, positive controls, failure injection, history completeness, and oracles—not only application tests. A checker that accepts a
     missing effect or omitted request invalidates the conclusions built on it.
